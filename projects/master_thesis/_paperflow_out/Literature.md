Acute coronary syndromes and plaque rupture remain a major clinical burden, motivating improved predictors of which lesions will cause events. Population-level statistics emphasize the scale of coronary disease [cite:go2012], and contemporary practice guidelines frame the clinical importance of identifying high-risk lesions in the management of acute coronary syndromes [cite:roffi2015]. Conceptual and clinicopathologic work has long emphasized the ‘‘vulnerable plaque’’ and the limitations of current screening to identify patients at imminent risk [cite:naghavi2003]. Prospective imaging studies have shown that events often arise from angiographically mild lesions, highlighting a gap between stenosis severity and rupture risk [cite:stone2011]. Autopsy and histopathologic investigations have further detailed mechanistic features of destabilization—necrotic core progression, intraplaque hemorrhage, neoangiogenesis, and localization of apoptotic macrophages at rupture sites—that underpin why mechanical failure of plaque caps is biologically plausible [cite:virmani2005] [cite:kolodgie2000].

Parallel to clinical and pathologic characterization, computational and biomechanical studies have examined coronary hemodynamics and vessel mechanics as tools to understand local drivers of stress and potential failure. Prior computational hemodynamics work has addressed coronary geometry and variable angulation in the left coronary system [cite:chaichana2011], and three-dimensional biomechanical analyses of coronary arteries provide a framework for quantifying mechanical fields relevant to plaque stress [cite:gholipour2018]. At the same time, cellular and tissue-scale contributors to arterial mechanics—such as the active contractile behavior of vascular smooth muscle and its pharmacologic modulation—inform how material properties and active tone can alter load-bearing behavior of the arterial wall and plaque-bearing segments [cite:brozovich2016].

Building on these clinical, pathologic, and computational precedents, this work uses a validated, cost-effective pulsatile fluid–structure interaction (FSI) approach to perform a 1,000-sample parametric study. By coupling large-sample parametric exploration with a fatigue-oriented metric (ΔPSS-based), we show that fatigue-perspective indicators align better with clinical notions of rupture risk than instantaneous peak metrics, and we quantify the dominant role of plaque and wall material properties in controlling these fatigue indicators. This positions our contribution as scaling biomechanical inference to clinically relevant sample sizes and reframing rupture propensity through a fatigue mechanics lens.

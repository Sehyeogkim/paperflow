Acute coronary syndromes caused by atherosclerotic plaque rupture remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, and contemporary clinical practice guidelines emphasize timely recognition and management of ST-elevation myocardial infarction [cite:members2008, cite:vandewerf2003]. Epidemiologic drivers such as type 2 diabetes amplify cardiovascular risk and the burden of atherosclerotic disease, motivating improved rupture-risk stratification beyond traditional risk factors [cite:martntimn2014].

Pathologic and biomechanical investigations have long sought the structural determinants of plaque stability. Classic pathological reviews and syntheses describe common structural features of unstable plaques and the interplay of mechanical loading and tissue weakening in precipitating disruption [cite:wal1999, cite:arroyo1999]. Structural stress analyses comparing ruptured and stable coronary lesions provided early empirical support for the hypothesis that elevated circumferential (hoop) stress localizes to rupture sites [cite:cheng1993], while work on experimental models highlights challenges and limitations in reproducing human plaque rupture in vivo [cite:cullen2003].

Parallel work has focused on hemodynamic drivers: image-based computational fluid dynamics and intravascular measurements have linked wall shear stress (WSS) extremes to plaque progression, composition, and distribution in coronary arteries, though the directionality of effect (high vs low WSS) remains debated [cite:eshtehardi2012, cite:meng2013]. Reviews and perspective pieces emphasize the central role of biomechanical forces throughout the natural history of coronary atherosclerosis and the need to integrate multiple mechanical metrics to predict events [cite:brown2016]. Building on these strands, the present study aims to integrate lumen-derived hemodynamic metrics and vessel-wall structural stress estimates in patient-specific data to better localize regions at elevated rupture risk; this addresses gaps left by small-sample structural analyses, conflicting WSS interpretations, and limitations of animal models by combining imaging, CFD, and structural modeling in the same lesions.
